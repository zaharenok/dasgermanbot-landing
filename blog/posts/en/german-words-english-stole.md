# German Words That English Stole and Pretended Were Its Own

English loves to borrow words. It took «beef» from French, «sushi» from Japanese, and an entire dictionary from German without ever saying thank you. Some of the most common English words you use every day were born in Germany. You just never noticed. Here are the German words that English stole and quietly pretended were its own.

## 1. Kindergarten

Every English speaker knows this word. Children go there. Parents talk about it. It sounds completely English. It is not. «Kindergarten» is German. «Kinder» means children. «Garten» means garden. A garden of children. Friedrich Frobel invented the concept in 1840. English borrowed it and never returned it. One of the most successful word heists in history.

## 2. Wanderlust

The English use «wanderlust» to sound poetic. Travel blogs love it. Instagram captions are full of it. But it is 100% German. «Wandern» means hiking. «Lust» means desire. The desire to wander. Germans invented the feeling and the word. English just copied both.

## 3. Schadenfreude

Taking pleasure in someone else's pain. Every language has this feeling. Only German has a word for it. English speakers loved it so much they just took the whole word. No translation attempt. No English equivalent. Just straight-up theft. «Schaden» means damage. «Freude» means joy. Damage-joy. The most honest word in any language.

## 4. Delicatessen

You buy sandwiches there. You say «deli» for short. But «Delicatessen» is German. «Delikat» means delicate or fine. «Essen» means food. Fine food. German immigrants opened these shops in New York in the 1800s. Americans loved the food so much they adopted the name. The word stayed.

## 5. Doppelganger

Your exact double. Your evil twin. The mysterious lookalike. «Doppelganger» comes from «Doppel» meaning double and «Ganger» meaning walker or goer. A double-walker. German literature made this word famous in the 1700s. English adopted it for its spooky precision. No English word captures it better.

## 6. Angst

Teenagers feel it. Existential philosophers analyze it. English speakers use it daily. «Angst» is German. It means fear, anxiety, or dread. Not just regular fear but deep, existential, soul-level fear. German philosophy gave English a word it did not know it needed.

## 7. Kaput

When something stops working, English speakers say «it's kaput.» They think it's slang. It's German. «Kaputt» means broken, destroyed, finished. Soldiers brought it back from World War II. It stuck. Now millions of English speakers use a German word when their phone dies.

## 8. Kitsch

Tacky art. Cheap souvenirs. Plastic flamingos on lawns. «Kitsch» is German. It describes art that is so bad it becomes entertaining. Germans invented the concept in the 1920s. The art world adopted the word because English had no equivalent. Still doesn't.

## 9. Spiel

«Give me your spiel.» Every salesperson has one. «Spiel» is German. It means play or game. In Yiddish-German it became a speech or pitch. English took it and made it mean «your rehearsed speech.» The word traveled from German theater to American boardrooms.

## 10. Ersatz

Fake. Inferior replacement. Not the real thing. «Ersatz» is German. It means substitute. During wartime, Germans used Ersatz coffee and Ersatz butter. The word entered English as a way to describe anything fake or second-rate. A word born from necessity, adopted by English for its precision.
