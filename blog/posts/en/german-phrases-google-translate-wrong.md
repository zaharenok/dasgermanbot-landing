# German Phrases That Google Translate Gets Completely Wrong

Google Translate is a miracle. It is also a liar. It will translate your German text into something that looks correct but means something completely different. You send a message thinking you said one thing. The German reads something else entirely. The consequences range from confusion to catastrophe. Here are the German phrases Google Translate butchers the most.

## 1. «Ich bin heiß»

Google Translate says: "I am hot." What it actually means: "I am horny." The correct phrase for feeling warm is «Mir ist heiß.» Google Translate does not distinguish between the two. Type «Ich bin heiß» into your phone and show it to a German on a summer day. Watch their face change. You did not complain about the weather. You announced your arousal. To a stranger.

## 2. «Ich bekomme ein Steak»

Google Translate says: "I become a steak." What you meant: "I'll have a steak." «Bekommen» means "to receive" or "to get" in German. It does NOT mean "to become." That word is «werden.» But Google Translate sees «bekomme» and sometimes goes full philosophical: you are transforming into a piece of meat. The waiter understands. Your dignity does not.

## 3. «Das ist mir Wurst»

Google Translate says: "That is sausage to me." What it actually means: "I don't care." This is a German idiom where "sausage" means "indifferent." Google Translate takes it literally. Now you are standing in a conversation talking about sausages when you meant to say you don't care. The German understands you. Google Translate does not.

## 4. «Jetzt haben wir den Salat»

Google Translate says: "Now we have the salad." What it actually means: "Now we're in trouble." Another idiom Google Translate murders. "Having the salad" in German means things went wrong. Google Translate thinks you're discussing lunch. You are not discussing lunch. You are expressing regret.

## 5. «Das ist nicht mein Bier»

Google Translate says: "That is not my beer." What it actually means: "That's not my problem." Germans use "Bier" metaphorically to mean "business" or "concern." Google Translate thinks you're talking about actual beer. You're not. You're saying you want nothing to do with this situation. Google Translate makes you sound like you're arguing about drinks.

## 6. «Ich habe die Nase voll»

Google Translate says: "I have the nose full." What it actually means: "I've had enough." The idiom means you're fed up. Done. Finished. Google Translate gives you a sentence about a full nose. Sounds like a medical condition. Is actually a declaration of frustration. Your German friend understands immediately. Google Translate sends you to the doctor.

## 7. «Das Leben ist kein Ponyhof»

Google Translate says: "Life is not a pony farm." What it actually means: "Life is not easy." Germans say this when someone complains too much. "Ponyhof" (pony farm) represents a fantasy world of easy living. Google Translate preserves the literal meaning perfectly — and misses the point entirely. You now sound like you're discussing equestrian real estate.

## 8. «Ich drücke dir die Daumen»

Google Translate says: "I press you the thumbs." What it actually means: "I'm keeping my fingers crossed for you." Germans press thumbs instead of crossing fingers for luck. Google Translate produces something that sounds vaguely threatening. Like you're going to physically compress someone's thumbs. You're wishing them luck. Google Translate makes it sound like a torture technique.

## 9. «Denk mal»

Google Translate says: "Think once." What it actually means: "Just think about it" or "What do you think?" It's casual, conversational. Google Translate turns it into a philosophical command. "Think once." As if you've never thought before. As if thinking is a one-time event. The German is just asking your opinion. Google Translate is demanding existential contemplation.

## 10. «Na ja»

Google Translate says: "Oh well" or "Yes." What it actually means: Everything and nothing. «Na ja» is the most versatile phrase in German. It can mean hesitation, disappointment, reluctant agreement, polite disagreement, or "I hear you but I disagree completely." Google Translate picks one meaning and runs with it. But «Na ja» is never just one thing. It's an entire conversation in two syllables. Google Translate will never understand this.
