# 20 German Words You Use Every Day Without Realizing It

You speak more German than you think. Not because you studied it — because German snuck into English centuries ago and never left. These words are so common you never questioned where they came from. Spoiler: Germany.

## 1. Kindergarten

Literally „children's garden." Friedrich Froebel invented the concept in 1840. The word spread worldwide. Every time you say „kindergarten," you're speaking German. Most people never notice.

## 2. Doppelganger

„Double-goer." A ghostly double of a living person. The concept exists in every culture, but only German gave it a word so perfect that English just took it. No translation needed.

## 3. Wanderlust

„Desire to wander." You've felt it — that urge to drop everything and travel. English has „itchy feet" but that's not nearly as poetic. German nailed it in one compound word.

## 4. Schadenfreude

We've covered this one before, but it deserves a repeat: „damage-joy." Taking pleasure in someone else's misfortune. English still has no native word for this. German does the emotional heavy lifting.

## 5. Rucksack

„Back-sack" (Rücken + Sack). Before „backpack" became standard, British English used „rucksack." The military still does. The word traveled with German soldiers and hikers and never left.

## 6. Delicatessen

From *Delikatessen* — „delicacies." You think of fancy sandwiches and potato salad. The original German referred to premium, prepared foods. American delis turned it into an entire cuisine category.

## 7. Pretzel

From *Brezel*, which comes from Latin *bracchiola* (little arms). But the twisted shape and the word both went through German first. Every soft pretzel you've ever eaten carries German DNA.

## 8. Spiel

In English, „spiel" means a rehearsed speech or pitch. In German, *das Spiel* simply means „game" or „play." The English meaning shifted to „a long, persuasive story" — probably from carnival barkers.

## 9. Kitsch

Tacky, gaudy, sentimental art. *Kitsch* is German, from the word *kitschen* (to smear). That ceramic cat on your grandma's shelf? That garden gnome? Kitsch. And the Germans invented the word for it too.

## 10. Angst

Fear, anxiety, existential dread. English uses „angst" for that specific teenage feeling of existential crisis. In German, *Angst* just means „fear," but English borrowed it for the philosophical version.

## 11. Kaput

From *kaputt* — broken, not working. „The computer is kaput." English speakers use it casually, not realizing it's perfectly standard German.

## 12. Ersatz

An inferior substitute. *Ersatzkaffee* was the substitute coffee Germans drank during wartime — made from acorns and grain. Now „ersatz" means anything fake or inferior.

## 13. Fest

As in festival, Oktoberfest, festiveness. English adopted *Fest* as a suffix: gabfest, snoozefest, lovefest. Every time you add „-fest" to something, you're speaking German.

## 14. Flak

From *Fliegerabwehrkanone* — „anti-aircraft gun." English adopted it as both a military term and a metaphor for criticism („taking flak"). The original word is a masterpiece of German compounding.

## 15. Gesundheit

„Health." You say it when someone sneezes. Everyone says it. Most people think it's just a sneeze word. It's German for „health," wishing the sneezer well.

## 16. Glock

As in the pistol. Gaston Glock, an Austrian engineer, invented it. The name is Germanic. Every action movie since 1982 has been speaking German.

## 17. Hinterland

„Behind-land." The area behind a coast or city. Used in geography, economics, and vaguely threatening government reports. Purely German.

## 18. Poltergeist

„Rumbling ghost." From *poltern* (to make noise) + *Geist* (ghost). German folklore gave the world the concept; German gave it the name.

## 19. Waltz

From *walzen* — to roll, to dance. The waltz was considered scandalous when it arrived in the 1800s — couples dancing face-to-face! The word, like the dance, came from Vienna.

## 20. Noodle

From *Nudel*. Yes, even „noodle" is German. The Italians have pasta, the Asians have noodles, but the English word „noodle" came from German. Your ramen is German-adjacent.
