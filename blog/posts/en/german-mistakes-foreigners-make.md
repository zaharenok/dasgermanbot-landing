# 15 German Mistakes That Instantly Give You Away as a Foreigner

You can study German for a decade. Live in Berlin. Marry a German. And then you say one word wrong and the illusion shatters. Germans are polite about it — they won't correct you. But they noticed. They always notice. Here are the mistakes that instantly mark you as non-native, and what to say instead.

## 1. Saying "Ich bin kalt" Instead of "Mir ist kalt"

"Ich bin kalt" means "I am a cold person" — emotionally cold, unfriendly. "Mir ist kalt" means "I feel cold." This is the #1 most common mistake and Germans hear it every time.

## 2. Mixing Up "Kennen" and "Wissen"

Both mean "to know" but they're not interchangeable. *Kennen* is for people, places, things you're familiar with. *Wissen* is for facts and information. "Ich kenne Berlin" = I've been there. "Ich weiss, wo Berlin ist" = I know where it is on a map.

## 3. Wrong Greeting for the Time of Day

"Guten Morgen" ends at 10 AM sharp. After that it's "Guten Tag." Saying "Guten Morgen" at 11:30 instantly sounds weird. Germans are precise about time — even in greetings.

## 4. Using "Wie geht es Sie?" Instead of "Wie geht es Ihnen?"

The formal "you" requires the dative: "Ihnen." "Wie geht es Sie" is grammatically wrong and sounds jarring to German ears. If you're going formal, go all the way.

## 5. Forgetting the Verb at the End

In subordinate clauses, the verb goes to the end. "Ich denke, dass du Recht hast" — not "Ich denke, dass du hast Recht." Forgetting this makes you sound like you're speaking English with German words.

## 6. Saying "Ich habe geheiratet mit"

In German, *heiraten* takes a direct object. "Ich habe meine Frau geheiratet" — no "mit." Adding "mit" is a direct translation from English "I married with someone" and it grates.

## 7. Capitalising Nouns Wrong

Every German noun is capitalised. Not just at the beginning of sentences — ALL of them. Writing "ich habe ein hund" instead of "ich habe ein Hund" immediately looks foreign.

## 8. Using "Machen" for Everything

"Machen" is not a universal verb. You don't "make a photo" (Foto machen) — you "take" it (fotografieren). You don't "make sport" (Sport machen is actually OK). But overusing "machen" sounds like you're avoiding proper verbs.

## 9. Pronouncing "CH" Like "SH"

"Ich" is not "ish." The German *ch* after front vowels (i, e, ä, ö, ü) is a soft sound made at the front of the mouth — not a French "sh." "ISH" is the quickest way to sound like a tourist.

## 10. Saying "Entschuldigung" Wrong

The "ch" in Entschuldigung is not "sh." And the "ung" at the end is not "oong." It's "oong" with a short u. Germans say it fast and clipped. Tourists say it slow and careful — and sound wrong.

## 11. Mixing Up "Dasselbe" and "Das Gleiche"

*Dasselbe* = the exact same object. *Das Gleiche* = the same kind of thing. "Wir haben dasselbe Auto" = we share one car. "Wir haben das gleiche Auto" = we each have the same model. Not the same.

## 12. Using "Viel" Instead of "Viele"

*Viel* is for uncountable nouns (viel Zeit, viel Wasser). *Viele* is for countable nouns (viele Leute, viele Bücher). "Viel Leute" is wrong and very noticeable.

## 13. Getting the Gender Wrong

Der, die, das. There's no shortcut. "Der Tisch" is masculine, "die Lampe" is feminine, "das Buch" is neuter. Getting the article wrong doesn't prevent understanding — but it immediately flags you as non-native.

## 14. Saying "Ich bin heiß" Instead of "Mir ist heiß"

Same problem as "kalt." "Ich bin heiß" in some contexts means "I am hot" in a suggestive way. "Mir ist heiß" means "I feel hot." Confuse these at your own risk.

## 15. Over-Apologizing

Germans don't say "sorry" every thirty seconds. One "Entschuldigung" is enough. Saying it repeatedly sounds insecure. German directness means: say it once, mean it, move on.
