# Moving to Germany in 2026: The Complete Survival Guide

You got the job. Or the university acceptance. Or you just decided that Germany sounds like a good idea. Congratulations. Now the real work begins.

Germany is wonderful. The bureaucracy that gets you in the door is... intense. But every single foreigner before you has survived it, and you will too. Here's the roadmap, in the order you actually need to do things.

## Step 1: Get Your Visa Sorted

If you're from the EU — skip this, you're free. Everyone else, listen up.

**Working?** You need a work visa (*Arbeitserlaubnis*) or Blue Card. The Blue Card requires a job offer with a minimum salary (currently around €43,800/year, less for shortage occupations like IT). Your employer usually helps with this.

**Studying?** Student visa (*Studentenvisum*). You need a university acceptance letter and proof you can support yourself (currently €11,208/year in a blocked account).

**Freelancing?** Freelance visa (*Freiberufler*). This one is harder. You need a business plan, proof of clients, and a lot of patience. Berlin is the easiest city for this.

**Pro tip:** Start the visa process 3-4 months before your move. German embassies are not known for speed.

## Step 2: Find an Apartment (Brace Yourself)

The German rental market is brutal, especially in Berlin, Munich, and Frankfurt. Here's what you need:

- **Schufa-Auskunft** — Your credit score. You won't have one when you arrive. Landlords know this. Some accept a letter from your employer instead.
- **Mietschuldenfreiheitsbescheinigung** — A certificate saying you don't owe rent to your previous landlord. You won't have this either.
- **Einkommensnachweis** — Proof of income. Last 3 payslips or a work contract.
- **Bürgschaft** — Sometimes a guarantor helps if you're missing the above.

**Where to look:** ImmobilienScout24, WG-Gesucht (for flatshares), Kleinanzeigen, Facebook groups. Expect to apply for 20-30 apartments before getting one.

**WG (Wohngemeinschaft):** A flatshare. Cheaper, easier to get, great for meeting people. Most young foreigners start here.

## Step 3: Anmeldung (City Registration)

This is step zero for everything else. Within 14 days of moving in, you must register your address at the local Bürgeramt (*Bürgeramt Anmeldung*).

**What to bring:**
- Passport
- Visa/residence permit
- Rental contract (*Mietvertrag*)
- Landlord confirmation (*Wohnungsgeberbestätigung*)
- Marriage certificate (if applicable)

**Book an appointment online.** Walk-ins are technically possible but you'll wait 4 hours. The appointment booking system (*Bürgeramt Termin*) is notoriously hard to navigate — slots open at midnight and disappear in minutes.

Once you have your *Anmeldebestätigung*, the bureaucratic floodgates open.

## Step 4: Health Insurance (Krankenversicherung)

Health insurance is mandatory in Germany. Not optional. Not "nice to have." Mandatory. You will be insured, or you will be fined.

**Public (Gesetzliche Krankenversicherung — GKV):** About 14.6% of your gross salary (split between you and your employer). Covers most things. TK (Techniker Krankenkasse), AOK, and Barmer are the biggest providers.

**Private (Private Krankenversicherung — PKV):** Only available if you earn above a threshold (~€66,150/year) or are self-employed. Can be cheaper when young but gets expensive as you age. Think carefully before choosing this.

**Students:** Pay a reduced rate of about €120/month. Total steal.

## Step 5: Open a Bank Account

You need a German bank account for rent, salary, and basically existing.

**Traditional banks:** Sparkasse, Volksbank, Commerzbank, Deutsche Bank. Require Anmeldung and sometimes a minimum deposit.

**Digital banks (easier for newcomers):** N26, Wise, Bunq, DKB. N26 is the most popular — you can open an account in 10 minutes from your phone, often before you even have your Anmeldung.

**Pro tip:** Get N26 first, use it while you sort out everything else, then decide if you want a traditional bank later.

## Step 6: Get a Phone Plan

**Prepaid:** Aldi Talk, Lidl Connect, Lebara — €10-15/month. No contract, no credit check. Perfect for your first weeks.

**Contract:** Telekom, Vodafone, O2 — €20-40/month with phone. Requires a German bank account and sometimes Schufa.

Most foreigners start prepaid and switch to a contract after 3-6 months.

## Step 7: Learn the Language (Seriously)

You can survive in Berlin with English. You cannot thrive in Germany with only English.

- Government offices: German only
- Doctors: Many speak English but not all
- Contracts and legal documents: German
- Your neighbors: German
- Making real German friends: German

**Free options:** DW (Deutsche Welle) courses, YouTube, language exchange meetups, your city's *Volkshochschule* (community adult education center).

## Step 8: Understand the Money

Germany runs on cash. Not metaphorically — literally. Many restaurants, bakeries, and small shops don't accept cards. Always carry €30-50 in cash.

Tipping: 5-10% is standard. Round up at restaurants. At bars, tell the bartender the total you want to pay, not the tip amount.

Taxes: Income tax ranges from 14% to 45%. Church tax (*Kirchensteuer*) is a real thing — 8-9% on top of income tax if you're registered with a church. You can opt out by formally leaving the church.

## The First Month Checklist

- [ ] Visa / residence permit
- [ ] Apartment (or WG room)
- [ ] Anmeldung at Bürgeramt
- [ ] Health insurance
- [ ] Bank account
- [ ] Phone plan
- [ ] Start learning German
- [ ] Figure out your local transport (Deutschlandticket = €58/month for all regional transit)
- [ ] Register at the local *Einwohnermeldeamt* if different from Anmeldung
- [ ] Get a library card (*Bibliotheksausweis*) — free internet, books, and sometimes language courses
