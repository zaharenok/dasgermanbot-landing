# German Apologies Ranked From Genuine to Pure Sarcasm

German has at least ten ways to say sorry. They look similar. They are not. Each one carries a different weight, a different tone, and a different level of sincerity. Using the wrong apology at the wrong time is a social error Germans notice immediately. Here is every German apology ranked from genuinely sorry to purely sarcastic.

## 1. «Es tut mir wirklich leid» — The Real Deal

Sincerity: 10/10. Translation: «I am truly sorry.» This is the heavy artillery of German apologies. Reserved for serious situations — hurting someone's feelings, causing real damage, making a significant mistake. The «wirklich» adds weight. When a German says this, they mean it completely. Do not waste this one on minor things. It loses power through overuse.

## 2. «Es tut mir leid» — Genuine

Sincerity: 8/10. Translation: «I am sorry.» Without the «wirklich» it is slightly lighter but still serious. You bumped into someone. You forgot an appointment. You said something insensitive. This apology carries real regret. Germans use it when they actually feel bad about something.

## 3. «Entschuldigung» — Standard

Sincerity: 6/10. Translation: «Excuse me / Apology.» The workhorse of German apologies. Used for small mistakes, getting attention, passing someone in a hallway. «Entschuldigung» is formal enough for strangers but casual enough for friends. It is the Swiss Army knife of saying sorry — works everywhere, means everything and nothing.

## 4. «Entschuldige» or «Entschuldige bitte» — Friendly

Sincerity: 5/10. The informal version. Dropping the «-ung» makes it casual. Adding «bitte» makes it softer. Used between friends, colleagues, family. «Entschuldige, kann ich mal vorbei?» at a crowded bar. Not a real apology. More like «pardon me, I exist near you.»

## 5. «Tschuldigung» — Lazy

Sincerity: 4/10. The swallowed version. The «En-» disappears entirely. What remains is «Tschuldigung» — a mumbled, half-pronounced acknowledgment that you might have done something mildly inconvenient. Common in Berlin, everywhere really. The speaker is not sorry. They are acknowledging the social requirement to say something.

## 6. «Sorry» — Borrowed

Sincerity: 3/10. English «sorry» has invaded German completely. Young Germans use it constantly. It is the lightest possible apology. You are 0.5 seconds late. You barely touched someone's shoulder. «Sorry» costs nothing and means almost nothing. It is the fast food of apologies — quick, cheap, and instantly forgotten.

## 7. «Pardon» — Vintage

Sincerity: 3/10. Borrowed from French, used primarily in southern Germany and Austria. «Pardon» is elegant but empty. It is what your grandmother says when she burps at dinner. Charming. Light. Not a real apology by any standard. Pure decoration.

## 8. «Verzeihung» — Dramatic

Sincerity: 2/10. No one says «Verzeihung» casually. It sounds like a line from a Goethe play. When someone says «Verzeihung» in real life, they are being either sarcastic or theatrical. «Oh, Verzeihung, habe ich Sie gestört?» means «I know I didn't disturb you and your reaction was unnecessary.» Pure passive aggression in vintage packaging.

## 9. «Oh, Entschuldigung!» with raised eyebrows — Sarcastic

Sincerity: 1/10. The tone destroys the words. «Oh, ENTSCHULDIGUNG!» with emphasis on the first syllable means the exact opposite of sorry. Someone bumped into YOU and now they are acting offended? «Oh, Entschuldigung!» The sarcasm is thick enough to spread on bread.

## 10. «Mein Beileid» — Not Even An Apology

Sincerity: 0/10. «Mein Beileid» means «my condolences.» It is used for death. If someone says «Mein Beileid» to your minor problem, they are telling you your complaint is so dramatic it might as well be a funeral. The most devastating German response to whining. Not sorry at all. Go away.
