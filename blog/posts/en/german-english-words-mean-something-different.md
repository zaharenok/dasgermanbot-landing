# German 'English' Words That Mean Something Completely Different

Germans love English. They sprinkle English words into every conversation. The problem? They use English words that mean something completely different from what they think. These are called pseudo-anglicisms — words that look English, sound English, and Germans are absolutely convinced ARE English. But no English speaker would ever use them this way. Here are the most confusing ones.

## 1. «Handy»

What Germans think it means: mobile phone. What it actually means in English: convenient, useful. «Kannst du mir dein Handy leihen?» (Can you lend me your mobile?) makes perfect sense in German. Say «Can you lend me your handy?» in London and you will get a very confused look. Germans are shocked every time they learn this. Their most common English word is not English at all.

## 2. «Beamer»

What Germans think it means: video projector. What it actually means in English: a BMW car (slang). Every German conference room has a «Beamer.» Every German asks «Ist der Beamer an?» (Is the projector on?) No English speaker calls a projector a «beamer.» This word exists purely in German imagination.

## 3. «Public Viewing»

What Germans think it means: watching a sports event together on a big screen in public. What it actually means in English: lying in state — the public display of a dead body before a funeral. During the World Cup, German cities proudly advertise «Public Viewing!» with thousands of cheering fans. An American reading this would think Germany is hosting a mass funeral. The horror is real.

## 4. «Bodybag»

What Germans think it means: a small bag worn across the body, a crossbody bag. What it actually means in English: a bag for a dead body. «Ich habe eine schöne neue Bodybag gekauft!» (I bought a beautiful new bodybag!) is a normal German sentence. An English speaker hears: «I purchased a lovely new corpse container.» Fashion or crime scene? You decide.

## 5. «Smoking»

What Germans think it means: a tuxedo, a dinner jacket. What it actually means in English: the act of smoking cigarettes. «Er trägt einen Smoking» (He's wearing a tuxedo) is completely normal in German. In English, «He's wearing a smoking» sounds like he's wrapped in cigarettes. The word comes from «smoking jacket» but Germans abbreviated it into pure confusion.

## 6. «Peeling»

What Germans think it means: an exfoliating skincare product. What it actually means in English: the act of removing skin from fruit or vegetables. «Ich brauch ein neues Peeling» (I need a new exfoliator) is normal in German. In English, asking for «a peeling» sounds like you want to remove your entire face. Dramatic skincare routine.

## 7. «Shooting»

What Germans think it means: a photo shoot. What it actually means in English: a gun attack. «Wir haben morgen ein Shooting» (We have a photo shoot tomorrow) is standard German fashion industry talk. In English, «We have a shooting tomorrow» means you should probably call the police. The fashion world or the crime world? Context is everything.

## 8. «Evergreen»

What Germans think it means: a classic song, movie, or idea that never gets old. What it actually means in English: a type of tree that stays green all year. «Das ist ein echter Evergreen!» (That's a real classic!) works in German. In English, calling a song «a real evergreen» sounds like you're describing a Christmas tree. Close enough that people understand, but wrong enough to be funny.

## 9. «Showmaster»

What Germans think it means: a TV show host. What it actually means in English: nothing. This word does not exist in English. «Showmaster» is a purely German invention combining «show» and «master» into a title no English speaker would recognize. The correct English term is «TV host» or «presenter.» Germans refuse to accept this.

## 10. «Oldtimer»

What Germans think it means: a vintage car. What it actually means in English: an elderly person. «Ich fahre einen Oldtimer» (I drive a vintage car) is a proud German statement. In English, «I drive an old-timer» sounds like you're chauffeuring your grandfather. The German love for vintage cars created a word that accidentally insults the elderly.
