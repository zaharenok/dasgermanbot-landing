# German Words That Mean the Opposite of What They Sound

German and English share the same language family. That means thousands of similar words. It also means thousands of traps. Words that look innocent in English but mean something completely different — sometimes dangerously different — in German. Here are the German words that mean the exact opposite of what they sound like to English speakers.

## 1. Gift

In English: a present. Something nice. In German: **poison**. One of the most dangerous false friends in existence. Walk into a German pharmacy and ask for a "Gift" and they will call emergency services. The actual word for present is **Geschenk**. Remember this one. It could save your life.

## 2. Brief

English: short underwear. German: **a letter**. Mail. Post. "Ich habe einen Brief bekommen" means someone got mail, not new underwear. The German word for briefs is **Unterhose**. This mix-up causes confusion in about every second conversation with expats.

## 3. Mist

English: fog. Atmospheric. German: **crap, manure, rubbish**. When a German says "Mist!" they are not admiring the weather. They are expressing frustration. Like saying "damn it." The German word for fog or mist is **Nebel**. Very different mood.

## 4. bald

English: no hair. German: **soon**. "Ich komme bald" means "I'll come soon," not "I'm coming hairless." The German word for bald is **kahl**. This one confuses every beginner at least once. Usually in a very awkward context.

## 5. also

English: too, as well. German: **so, therefore, well then**. "Also..." at the start of a German sentence means "So..." not "Also..." When a German says "Also, das ist gut" they mean "So, that's good." Not "Also, that's good." The German word for also/too is **auch**.

## 6. fast

English: quick, rapid. German: **almost, nearly**. "Ich bin fast fertig" doesn't mean "I'm quick and finished." It means "I'm almost done." The German word for fast/quick is **schnell**. This causes real problems when someone says "fast fertig" and you think the work is done.

## 7. bekommen

Looks like "become." Sounds like "become." Means **to get, receive**. "Was möchten Sie bekommen?" means "What would you like to get?" not "What would you like to become?" The German word for become is **werden**. This is probably the most common false friend mistake.

## 8. Chef

English: cook in a restaurant. German: **boss**. Your German Chef doesn't cook anything. They sign your paycheck. "Mein Chef ist im Urlaub" means your boss is on vacation, not that the kitchen is closed. The German word for a cook is **Koch** (male) or **Köchin** (female).

## 9. Rat

English: rodent. German: **advice, council**. "Ich brauche einen Rat" means "I need advice," not "I need a rat." The German word for the animal rat is also **Ratte** — with an extra syllable. One letter changes everything from wisdom to pest.

## 10. kind

English: nice, sort. German: **child**. "Das Kind weint" means "the child is crying," not "the sort is nice." The German word for kind/nice is **nett** or **freundlich**. The German word for sort/type is **Art** or **Sorte**. Complete opposite territory.
