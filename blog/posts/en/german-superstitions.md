# 13 German Superstitions People Actually Believe

Germans are rational. Logical. Scientific. They also knock on wood three times, spit over their shoulder, and refuse to toast with water. Superstitions in Germany aren't just folklore — they're daily habits that millions follow without thinking. Some are charming. Some are bizarre. All of them are taken more seriously than you'd expect from a nation of engineers.

## 1. Seven Years of Bad Sex (Prost ohne Augenkontakt)

Clink glasses without looking the other person in the eye? Seven years of bad sex. This isn't a joke. Germans actually believe this. At every party, every bar, every gathering — people lock eyes when saying "Prost." Every single time. No exceptions.

## 2. No Birthday Wishes Before the Actual Day

Wishing someone happy birthday early is not thoughtful — it's a curse. Germans genuinely believe that early wishes bring bad luck. Wait until midnight. Or the actual day. A colleague once left a card on my desk at 11:58 PM on a video call. That's commitment to avoiding bad luck.

## 3. Touching Red When Two People Say the Same Word

Two people say the same thing at the same time? Both must touch something red immediately. No red nearby? Touch your own nose. Why? Because saying the same word simultaneously means you'll otherwise fight or something terrible will happen. Obviously.

## 4. Schadenfreude Is Bad Luck

Feeling happy about someone else's misfortune invites the same fate upon yourself. The Germans literally have a word for taking joy in others' suffering (Schadenfreude) but they also believe the universe will punish you for it. Karma, German-style.

## 5. Never Toast with Water

Raising a glass of water to say "Prost" means you're wishing death upon everyone at the table. Toasting with water symbolizes the dead. At a German dinner, if you're not drinking alcohol, order a Sprite. Anything but water for toasting.

## 6. Toi Toi Toi (Spitting for Luck)

Before an exam, a performance, a job interview — Germans say "Toi toi toi" and knock on wood three times. "Toi toi toi" represents three spits over the left shoulder to ward off evil spirits. Actors whisper it before going on stage. It's the German "break a leg."

## 7. Finding a Spider in the Morning Brings Money

See a spider before noon? Good luck — money is coming. See it in the evening? Bad luck. This is why German grandmothers refuse to kill spiders before lunchtime. The spider is an ATM until 12:00.

## 8. Chimney Sweeps Bring Good Luck

A Schornsteinfeger (chimney sweep) on your street means good luck is coming. If one crosses your path, you should touch a button on your clothing for extra fortune. Some Germans still touch their jacket button when they see a sweep.

## 9. Don't Sweep Over Someone's Feet

If a broom sweeps over your feet while someone is cleaning, you will apparently never marry. Germans take this seriously enough to lift their feet when someone is sweeping nearby. Even in public. Even at age 40.

## 10. Losing a Wedding Ring Means the Marriage Ends

Dropping or losing your wedding ring is considered a sign that the marriage will fail. Not metaphorically. Actually. German jewelers report that lost-ring customers look genuinely distressed — and not just because of the gold price.

## 11. Salt Spilled? Throw It Over Your Left Shoulder

Spilling salt brings bad luck. The fix: immediately throw a pinch over your left shoulder. This dates back to medieval times when salt was expensive. The left shoulder throw blinds the devil waiting behind you. Germans still do this. At restaurants.

## 12. Friday the 13th Is Real

Germans take Friday the 13th seriously enough that hospitals report fewer scheduled surgeries on that day. Some buildings skip the 13th floor. Hotel rooms skip number 13. This isn't unique to Germany — but the intensity might be.

## 13. Fingers Crossed Is Not Universal

In Germany, "fingers crossed" (die Daumen drücken) literally means "pressing your thumbs." Germans press their thumbs into their fists and say "Ich drück dir die Daumen" — "I press my thumbs for you." Crossing fingers is understood but less authentic.
