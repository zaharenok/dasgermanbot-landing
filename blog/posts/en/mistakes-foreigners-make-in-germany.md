# 15 Mistakes Foreigners Make in Germany (And How to Fix Them)

Picture this. You just moved to Berlin. You're feeling confident, maybe a little smug. Then a stranger on the street yells at you for jaywalking. The cashier glares because you tried to make small talk. And you can't buy a bread roll on Sunday because, well, *Sunday is Sunday*.

Germany is wonderful. It's also a country with unspoken rules that everyone somehow knows — except you.

Here are 15 mistakes almost every foreigner makes, and exactly how to fix them.

## 1. Crossing the Street on Red

You're standing at a crosswalk. It's 11 PM. No car in sight. The light is red. You step off the curb — and a German pedestrian clears their throat loud enough to wake the neighbors.

Germans wait. Always. At 3 AM with zero traffic, they wait. Kids are watching, the logic goes. And honestly? It grows on you.

**The fix:** Just wait. Stand there. Embrace the stillness. Think about your life choices. The light will turn green eventually.

## 2. Being "Five Minutes Late"

In many cultures, "let's meet at 7" means 7:15ish. In Germany, 7 means 7:00. Not 7:02. Not "I'm on my way" when you haven't left yet. Seven o'clock.

If you're meeting a German friend and you're late, they will worry. If you're meeting a German colleague and you're late, they will notice. If you're meeting a German doctor and you're late, your appointment is gone.

**The fix:** Arrive five minutes early. If something delays you, send a message *before* you're late, not after.

## 3. Not Separating Your Trash

Germany takes recycling personally. You don't just throw things "in the bin." There are bins. Plural. Paper, plastic, glass (sorted by color), organic waste, residual waste — and if you put a yogurt cup in the paper bin, someone *will* notice.

Your landlord might leave a passive-aggressive note. Your neighbors might stage an intervention. The bio bin has feelings.

**The fix:** Learn the basic categories. *Gelber Sack* for packaging, blue bin for paper, brown bin for organic, black bin for everything else. Glass goes to the public glass containers sorted by color. When in doubt, ask — Germans love explaining their trash system.

## 4. Making Small Talk with the Cashier

"Hi! How's your day going? Crazy weather, right?" — This is friendly in many countries. In a German supermarket, it's deeply confusing.

Cashiers are efficient. They scan, you pay, you leave. The transaction takes 45 seconds. They don't want to hear about your weekend. You don't want to hear about theirs. Everyone is at peace with this arrangement.

**The fix:** Say "Hallo" and "Danke" — that's the full acceptable script. Maybe "Tschüss" on the way out if you're feeling bold. Anything beyond that is between you and your therapist.

## 5. Being Loud on Sunday

Sunday in Germany is a *Ruhetag* — a day of rest. And "rest" means silence. No drilling. No mowing the lawn. No vacuuming at 2 PM. No washing machine. Your neighbors take this seriously, and they *will* knock on your door.

Sunday is for reading, walking, brunch, and contemplating existence. Preferably in that order.

**The fix:** Do your noisy chores on Saturday. On Sunday, keep it down. If you absolutely must do laundry, do it quietly and live with the guilt.

## 6. Getting Sie and du Wrong

German has two ways to say "you" — formal (*Sie*) and informal (*du*). Using the wrong one is awkward at best, insulting at worst.

Walk into a shop and say "Wie geht's?" to a 60-year-old cashier? Too informal. Call your new colleague *Sie* after they've offered *du*? Now you seem stiff and distant.

**The fix:** Default to *Sie* with strangers, especially anyone older or in a professional setting. Wait for them to offer the *du* — they'll say "Wir können uns duzen" or just start using *du* with you. Once the *du* is offered, switching back to *Sie* would be bizarre.

## 7. Not Carrying Cash

You're at a bakery. You order a pretzel. You pull out your card. The baker stares at you like you've committed a crime.

Germany runs on cash. Many restaurants, bakeries, and small shops don't accept cards. Even places that do might have a minimum amount or a broken card reader that's been "broken" since 2019.

**The fix:** Always carry at least 30-50 euros in cash. Coins too — you'll need them for shopping carts, bathrooms, and the bakery.

## 8. Wearing Shoes Indoors

You walk into a German apartment with your shoes on. The host looks at your feet. Then at you. Then back at your feet. The silence says everything.

Keeping shoes on inside is considered gross in Germany. Floors are clean. People sit on the floor sometimes. Babies crawl around. Your street shoes don't belong on that surface.

**The fix:** Take your shoes off at the door. Some hosts will offer you slippers (*Hausschuhe*). Accept them. It's cozy, and your feet will thank you.

## 9. Wrong Bakery Etiquette

German bakeries are sacred institutions. But the first time you walk in, the system feels chaotic. No clear line. Everyone shouting their orders. The baker moving at light speed.

Stand there looking confused for too long and you'll be skipped. Twice.

**The fix:** Know what you want before you approach the counter. Say your order clearly and quickly. Take a receipt (*Quittung*) if they offer one. Pay in cash (see mistake 7). And never, ever try to cut the invisible line — Germans have a sixth sense for that.

## 10. Taking German Directness Personally

A German colleague says "That's a bad idea." A German friend tells you your presentation had problems. Your German host asks why you haven't finished your food.

None of this is rude. Germans communicate directly. They say what they mean. They think sugarcoating is dishonest. If they criticize your idea, it's about the idea — not about you.

**The fix:** Don't take it personally. It's not hostility. It's efficiency. A German who tells you honestly what they think is a German who respects you enough not to waste your time.

## 11. Not Bringing Your Own Bags to the Supermarket

You get to the checkout. Your groceries pile up. The cashier scans everything at warp speed. "Haben Sie eine Tasche?" they ask. You don't. Now you're buying bags you didn't plan on, or carrying your groceries home like a human pyramid.

**The fix:** Keep a folded cloth bag in your jacket, backpack, or purse. Always. It costs one euro and saves you from the shame of juggling eggs on the U-Bahn.

## 12. Hugging When You Should Shake Hands

In many cultures, hugging is the standard greeting. In Germany? Not so much. Especially on a first meeting, going in for a hug when the other person is extending a hand creates that horrible moment where you bump chests and accidentally touch faces.

Germans shake hands. Firm, brief, with eye contact. It's professional, it's clear, and there's zero ambiguity about what's happening.

**The fix:** Shake hands. Save hugs for close friends — and even then, ask first or let them initiate. Some Germans hug. Some don't. When in doubt, extend your hand.

## 13. Forgetting to Say "Mahlzeit"

It's lunchtime at the office. You walk past a colleague in the hallway. You say "Hi!" They look at you like something is missing.

That missing thing is *Mahlzeit* — literally "meal time," but used as a general lunch greeting in workplaces across Germany. It's the equivalent of "enjoy your lunch" condensed into one word.

**The fix:** Around noon, swap your usual greeting for "Mahlzeit!" It works as hello, goodbye, and general acknowledgment that it's lunch o'clock. You'll sound like you've been working there for years.

## 14. Queue Jumping (or Not Noticing the Queue)

Germans don't always form obvious lines. At the bakery, the butcher, or the bus stop, the queue might be an abstract concept — a loose gathering of people who all silently know who was first.

If you walk up and start ordering before someone who's been waiting, the death stares will be immediate. And justified.

**The fix:** When you arrive, make eye contact and ask "Sind Sie dran?" (Are you next?) or "Wer war als Erstes da?" (Who was here first?). This shows respect for the invisible queue and buys you social immunity.

## 15. Ordering Beer Wrong

You're at a bar. You say "Ein Bier, bitte." The bartender gives you whatever's on tap. It might be a Pils. It might be a Helles. It might be something you've never heard of that tastes like it was brewed in a cave.

Germany has over 7,000 beers. Every region has its own. Ordering "a beer" is like walking into a French restaurant and asking for "some wine."

**The fix:** Know your preferences. *Pils* is crisp and bitter. *Helles* is smooth and malty. *Weizen* is a wheat beer, cloudy and fruity. *Kölsch* is light and served in tiny glasses in Cologne. *Alt* is dark and served in Düsseldorf. Order by name, and if you're not sure, ask what they have on tap — "Was haben Sie vom Fass?"
