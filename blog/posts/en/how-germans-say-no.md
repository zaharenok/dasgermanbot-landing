# How Germans Actually Say 'No': 20 Polite Rejections You'll Hear

A German will sooner drive 200 km out of their way than say the word "nein" to your face. Not because they're weak. Because direct rejection feels rude. Instead, they've developed an entire language of soft no's that sound like maybe's but mean absolutely not.

If you're learning German, understanding these phrases is survival. Here are 20 ways Germans say no without saying no — ranked from softest to most final.

## The Soft No's (You Think It's a Yes)

### 1. "Das schauen wir mal"
Literally: "We'll see about that." Translation: No. This is the classic German maybe-that's-actually-a-no. If a German says this, they have already decided no in their head.

### 2. "Mal sehen"
"Let's see." Sounds open-minded. It's not. They've seen. The answer is no.

### 3. "Das ist eine gute Frage"
"That's a good question." They're not complimenting you. They're buying time because they don't want to answer. The answer is probably no.

### 4. "Wir müssen das erst besprechen"
"We need to discuss this first." With whom? Everyone. Anyone. Just not you. Not now. Probably not ever.

### 5. "Ich muss mal nachfragen"
"I need to check." They don't need to check anything. They need to say no without making eye contact.

## The Medium No's (You're Starting to Worry)

### 6. "Das ist schwierig"
"That's difficult." In German, calling something "schwierig" is a death sentence for your request. Nothing difficult will ever become easy.

### 7. "Da müssen wir schauen, ob das geht"
"We'll have to see if that's possible." It's not possible. They already know. But they'll pretend to investigate.

### 8. "Im Prinzip ja, aber..."
"In principle yes, but..." Any sentence that starts with "im Prinzip ja" ends in no. The "but" is the entire point.

### 9. "Das wird leider nicht ganz so einfach"
"Unfortunately that won't be quite so simple." Nothing in German bureaucracy is simple. This phrase means it's impossible and they feel mildly sorry for you.

### 10. "Ich würde ja gerne, aber..."
"I would love to, but..." They would not love to. The "but" is doing all the work.

## The Firm No's (They're Serious Now)

### 11. "Das geht leider nicht"
"Unfortunately that doesn't work." The word "leider" (unfortunately) is doing emotional labor. The answer is no, but they want you to know they feel bad about it.

### 12. "Das ist leider nicht möglich"
"Unfortunately that's not possible." Same energy as #11, but with the authority of a bureaucrat. This one comes with a slight shoulder shrug.

### 13. "Da kann ich Ihnen leider nicht weiterhelfen"
"Unfortunately I can't help you with that." You'll hear this at the Bürgeramt. You'll hear this a lot. It means go away.

### 14. "Das steht leider nicht zur Debatte"
"Unfortunately that's not up for debate." This is a German locking the door. Politely.

### 15. "Da muss ich Ihnen einen Korb geben"
"I have to give you a basket." Yes, "einen Korb geben" — literally "give a basket" — means to reject someone. Dating, job applications, proposals. Basket = no.

## The Final No's (Absolutely Not, Forever)

### 16. "Kommt nicht in Frage"
"Out of the question." No negotiation. No discussion. The door is closed, locked, and the key has been swallowed.

### 17. "Auf keinen Fall"
"Under no circumstances." This is the closest thing to a direct "nein" you'll get in polite company.

### 18. "Da rede ich mir den Mund nicht fusselig"
"I won't talk my mouth ragged about that." Meaning: I will not discuss this further, ever. A beautiful idiom for an absolute shutdown.

### 19. "Das ist mir zu umständlich"
"That's too complicated for me." Germans hate unnecessary complexity. If your request is "umständlich," it's dead.

### 20. "Nein."
Yes, sometimes they do just say "nein." Usually after they've tried all 19 other options first. When you finally hear a direct "nein," know that you've pushed through 19 layers of polite resistance. Respect.
