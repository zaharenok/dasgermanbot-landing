# German Words You Need At The Apotheke To Survive In Germany

You are sick in Germany. Your head is exploding. Your throat is on fire. You drag yourself to the nearest store with a pharmacy-sounding name. You walk into a dm. You ask for painkillers. The employee looks at you with genuine pity and says: «Das ist eine Drogerie.» You have made the most common mistake in German healthcare. You have confused a Drogerie with an Apotheke. Here are the words that will save you next time.

## 1. «Apotheke» — The Real Pharmacy

The red A sign. The place with actual medicine. Real drugs. Professional pharmacists in white coats. The Apotheke is where you go when you are genuinely sick. Only the Apotheke sells real medication. Aspirin, ibuprofen, prescription drugs — all here. There is probably one on every corner. Look for the red «A» sign. Walk toward it. Do not stop at dm.

## 2. «Drogerie» — NOT A Pharmacy

Dm. Rossmann. These are Drogerien. Drugstores. They sell shampoo, snacks, vitamins, and basically everything except actual medicine. You cannot buy real painkillers here. No ibuprofen. No aspirin. Just homeopathic nonsense and gummy vitamins. Every foreigner in Germany has made this mistake. Once. You learn fast when your head is pounding.

## 3. «Rezept» — The Prescription

A doctor's prescription. «Haben Sie ein Rezept?» (Do you have a prescription?) is the first question at the Apotheke counter. If you have one, you get the medicine. If you don't, you get whatever is available without one. The German healthcare system runs on Rezepte. Lose your Rezept and you are going back to the doctor. No exceptions.

## 4. «Rezeptfrei» — Without Prescription

Literally: prescription-free. Medicine you can buy without seeing a doctor. Ibuprofen, paracetamol, throat lozenges, basic cold medicine. «Haben Sie etwas rezeptfreies gegen Kopfschmerzen?» (Do you have something without prescription for headaches?) This phrase is your best friend. Memorize it. It works.

## 5. «Verschreibungspflichtig» — Prescription Required

The opposite of rezeptfrei. This 22-letter monster means the medicine requires a prescription. You cannot buy it over the counter. No amount of begging will help. The pharmacist will not bend the rules. This is Germany. Rules are rules. Go to the doctor first. Get your Rezept. Come back. In that order.

## 6. «Schmerzmittel» — Painkillers

Literally: pain-middle. «Schmerz» is pain. «Mittel» means means or agent. Together: pain-agent. Painkillers. The most requested item in any Apotheke. «Ich brauche Schmerzmittel» (I need painkillers) will get you ibuprofen or paracetamol. The pharmacist will ask: «Ibuprofen oder Paracetamol?» Pick one. Neither will disappoint you.

## 7. «Tabletten» — Pills

«Nehmen Sie zwei Tabletten dreimal täglich» (Take two pills three times daily). The standard German medical instruction. Tabletten are pills. They come in blister packs that are impossible to open when you are already sick. German engineering at its finest.

## 8. «Salbe» — Ointment

For everything external. Cuts, burns, rashes, mysterious skin problems. «Haben Sie eine Salbe gegen...» (Do you have an ointment for...) followed by pointing at the problem area works perfectly when you don't know the German word for your specific affliction. The pharmacist has seen everything. Nothing surprises them.

## 9. «Nebenwirkungen» — Side Effects

Literally: next-effects. The side effects. Every medicine in Germany comes with a massive information leaflet listing every possible Nebenwirkung. Headache, nausea, dizziness, and «in rare cases, death.» Germans read these leaflets carefully. Then they take the medicine anyway. The leaflet is half the Apotheke experience.

## 10. «Dosierung» — Dosage

How much to take. The Dosierung is printed on every package and leaflet. «Was ist die richtige Dosierung?» (What is the correct dosage?) is a question that proves you are responsible. German pharmacists appreciate this. They will explain the Dosierung in detail. Possibly with a diagram. Possibly twice.
