# German Phrases That Instantly Reveal You're a Foreigner

You studied German. You know the grammar. You can order coffee. But then you say «Ich bin heiß» and every German in the room knows immediately — you are not from here. The mistake is invisible to you. Obvious to them. German has phrases that work differently from English, and using the English logic in German exposes you faster than any accent. Here are the phrases that instantly reveal you're a foreigner.

## 1. «Ich bin heiß» — I am hot

In English, this is fine. In German, «Ich bin heiß» means «I am attractive» or «I am horny.» What you mean is «Mir ist heiß» — literally «to me it is hot.» Germans use the dative «mir» for physical sensations. Every other option sends the wrong message. This mistake is the number one foreigner giveaway.

## 2. «Ich bin gut» — I am good

When someone asks «Wie geht's?» and you say «Ich bin gut,» Germans hear «I am good at something» or «I am a good person.» The correct answer is «Mir geht es gut» or simply «Gut.» The «mir» construction strikes again. Your physical and emotional states belong to «mir,» not «ich.»

## 3. «Ich bin fertig» — I am finished

This actually works in some contexts. But «Ich bin fertig» can also mean «I am exhausted» or «I am done mentally.» If you want to say you finished a task, say «Ich bin fertig» with the right context. Or better: «Ich habe fertig» or «Ich bin damit fertig.» Nuance matters.

## 4. «Ich habe Hunger» vs «Ich bin hungrig»

Both are correct. But «Ich habe Hunger» is what Germans actually say. «Ich bin hungrig» sounds like a textbook. Nobody says that in real life. Using textbook German instead of spoken German is another way foreigners expose themselves. Learn what people say, not what the book teaches.

## 5. «Ich bin kalt» — I am cold

Same as hot. «Ich bin kalt» means «I am a cold person» — emotionally cold, unfeeling. What you want is «Mir ist kalt.» The pattern is always the same: physical sensations take «mir,» personality traits take «ich.» Mix them up and you accidentally describe your personality instead of your body temperature.

## 6. «Wirst du kommen?» — Will you come?

The word order is wrong. In German, the verb goes to position two in questions with a question word, but in yes/no questions the verb comes first: «Kommst du?» Or with future tense: «Wirst du kommen?» actually works, but Germans rarely use the future tense this way. They just say «Kommst du?» Simple present. Overcomplicating is a foreigner habit.

## 7. «Ich arbeite für eine Firma» — I work for a company

Not wrong grammatically. But nobody says «Firma» when describing their employer casually. Germans say «Ich arbeite bei...» followed by the company name. «Ich arbeite bei Siemens.» Generic «Firma» sounds like you learned German from a 1990s textbook.

## 8. «Was ist dein Name?» — What is your name?

The grammar is English logic applied to German. Correct is «Wie heißt du?» or «Wie ist dein Name?» The question word is «wie» (how), not «was» (what). Germans ask «how are you called,» not «what is your name.» This tiny mistake screams foreigner.

## 9. «Ich bin langweilig» — I am boring

You meant «I am bored.» The difference: «Ich bin langweilig» means «I am a boring person.» «Mir ist langweilig» means «I am bored.» The «mir» pattern again. It is the single most important grammar pattern for sounding German. Miss it and everyone knows.

## 10. «Guten Tag» as your only greeting

«Guten Tag» is correct. It is also formal, stiff, and nobody under 40 uses it in casual settings. Real Germans say «Hallo,» «Moin,» «Grüß Gott» in Bavaria, or just «Na?» «Guten Tag» is what language apps teach. «Na» is what Germans actually say. The gap between textbook German and street German is enormous.
