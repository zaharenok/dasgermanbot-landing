# German Work Culture Shocks That Hit Every Foreigner

You got the job. You signed the contract. You speak enough German to survive. Nothing prepared you for what actually happens in a German office. The rules are unspoken but absolute. Break one and you'll feel it — not through confrontation, but through silence and stares. Here are the workplace shocks every foreigner experiences.

## 1. The Naked Lunch Break

Germans eat lunch together. At a shared table. You sit down, unpack your meal, and everyone sees what you brought. No hiding behind your desk. No eating alone at your screen. Communal lunch is sacred. And yes, they will judge your Tupperware.

## 2. No Small Talk Before Meetings

The meeting starts at 9:00. At 9:00 you talk business. No "how was your weekend?" No "nice weather." Germans consider pre-meeting small talk as time theft. Get to the point. Immediately.

## 3. Sick? Bring a Doctor's Note

Missing one day of work? You need a doctor's note (Arbeitsunfähigkeitsbescheinigung). Not for a week. For ONE day. The name of the document alone is longer than your illness. This is non-negotiable.

## 4. Title Usage Is Serious

If someone has a PhD, you call them "Herr Doktor Schmidt." Not Herr Schmidt. Not "hey." The title is part of their name. In emails, in person, always. Ignoring academic titles is considered deeply disrespectful.

## 5. The Coffee Machine Hierarchy

The coffee machine has rules. The boss doesn't make coffee for the team. The newest person doesn't get served. Everyone makes their own. But — you must ask if anyone else wants one when you go to the machine. That's the rule.

## 6. Email Formality Is Extreme

Opening an email with "Hi" is too casual for most German offices. "Sehr geehrte Frau Müller" is standard. Even between colleagues who've worked together for five years. First names? That requires explicit permission.

## 7. Vacation Is Sacred and Non-Negotiable

Germans get 30 days of vacation and they use ALL of them. Taking vacation is not a sign of laziness — it's a right. Nobody will admire you for skipping vacation. They'll think something is wrong with you.

## 8. You Cannot Be Fired — But You Can Be Ignored

German labor law makes firing extremely difficult. But the German passive-aggressive silence is worse than any confrontation. Colleagues stop including you. Meetings happen without you. You exist — but you don't.

## 9. Separation of Work and Life Is Absolute

Do not email your colleague at 8 PM. Do not call on Saturday. Do not discuss work at the company party (yes, this is ironic). Germans guard their free time with the intensity of a castle guard. Respect it.

## 10. Direct Feedback Is Not Rudeness

Your German colleague says your presentation "was not good." No sugar-coating. No "great job but maybe consider..." Just honesty. This is not personal. It's efficiency. Germans believe polite dishonesty is worse than uncomfortable truth.

## 11. The Door Must Be Closed

German office doors are closed. Always. An open door means "come in." A closed door means "do not disturb." There is no half-open. No knock-and-enter. Closed means closed.

## 12. Beers at Work Events Are Mandatory Fun

Sommerfest. Weihnachtsfeier. The company party has beer. You are expected to drink. Not getting drunk — but having one beer is social participation. Refusing entirely raises eyebrows. "Alkoholfrei" beer exists for this exact reason.

## 13. Print Everything

Germans love printed documents. Contracts, meeting notes, presentations. Digital-only is suspicious. If it's not on paper, it didn't happen. The printer is the most important machine in any German office.
