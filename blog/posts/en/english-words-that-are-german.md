# English Words That Are Actually German: You Know More Than You Think

You've been speaking German this whole time.

Not fluently, obviously. But scattered throughout your everyday English vocabulary are dozens of words that were born in Berlin, raised in Munich, and shipped across the Atlantic without so much as a thank-you card. You use them at work, at bars, on social media, in therapy. You probably used one today without realizing it.

The truth is, English borrowed shamelessly from German. And the best part? Once you see the pattern, you'll realize your brain already has a head start on learning German. You just didn't know it.

Let's count the words you already know.

## The Ones You Use Every Single Day

**Kindergarten.** You probably went to one. The word literally means "children's garden" — *Kinder* (children) + *Garten* (garden). Friedrich Froebel coined it in 1840, and English just... took it.

**Hamburger.** Named after the city of Hamburg. Originally "Hamburg steak" — a patty of ground beef served to German sailors and emigrants. The sandwich came later, but the name stuck.

**Frankfurter.** Same deal. From Frankfurt. A sausage from Frankfurt. That's it. That's the whole story.

**Pretzel.** From the German *Brezel*, which itself probably comes from the Latin *bracchiolum* (little arms) because pretzels look like folded arms. You've been eating etymology this whole time.

**Noodle.** From the German *Nudel*. Yep. One of the most basic food words in English is German.

## The Feeling Words

German has a strange talent for naming emotions that English can't quite pin down. So English just gave up and borrowed them.

**Angst.** In German, it means "fear" or "anxiety." In English, it picked up a slightly cooler, more existential vibe. Teenagers love it. Therapists use it. It's everywhere.

**Schadenfreude.** This is the big one. The pleasure you feel when someone else trips, fails, or gets a parking ticket. *Schaden* (damage) + *Freude* (joy). Damage-joy. The Germans literally invented a word for being a little bit terrible, and English speakers loved it so much they kept it unchanged.

**Wanderlust.** The irresistible urge to travel and explore. *Wandern* (to hike) + *Lust* (desire). If you've ever quit your job to backpack through Southeast Asia, you've experienced *Wanderlust*.

**Weltanschauung.** A comprehensive worldview or philosophy of life. *Welt* (world) + *Anschauung* (view/outlook). It sounds pretentious, which is exactly why English uses it.

## The Spooky and the Strange

**Doppelgänger.** A ghostly double of a living person. *Doppel* (double) + *Gänger* (goer/walker). Your evil twin? That's a *Doppelgänger*.

**Poltergeist.** A noisy, mischievous ghost. *Poltern* (to make noise/to rumble) + *Geist* (ghost/spirit). So a poltergeist is literally a "rumbling ghost." Makes sense.

**Kitsch.** Cheap, tacky, sentimental art or decoration. The kind of stuff your grandma collects — porcelain figurines, velvet paintings, motivational cat posters. German didn't just give us the objects, it gave us the perfect insult for them.

**Kaput.** From the German *kaputt*, meaning broken, ruined, done for. You dropped your phone and the screen shattered? It's kaput. Your car engine made a weird noise and died? Kaput. Your motivation on a Monday morning? Absolutely kaput.

## The Words You Didn't Expect

Some German loanwords hide in plain sight. You'd never guess their origin.

**Rucksack.** From *Rücken* (back) + *Sack* (bag). A bag for your back. A backpack. The British still use "rucksack" regularly.

**Delicatessen.** From *Delikatessen*, plural of *Delikatesse* (delicacy). That fancy sandwich shop? German.

**Lager.** As in lager beer. From the German *Lager* meaning "storeroom" or "warehouse." It refers to beer that's been stored (lagered) at cold temperatures for aging. Next time you order a lager, you're ordering storage.

**Pilsner.** A type of pale lager named after the Czech city of Plzeň (Pilsen in German). The style was developed by a Bavarian brewer named Josef Groll in 1842. Your favorite Friday-night beer has German-Czech roots.

**Schnitzel.** From *schnitzen* (to carve/cut). A thin, breaded, fried meat cutlet. If you've ever had one, you understand why the word crossed borders.

**Flak.** As in "taking flak" (criticism). Originally an acronym: *Fliegerabwehrkanone* — anti-aircraft gun. English soldiers in WWII started using it, and eventually it became a metaphor for any incoming heat.

**Ersatz.** Meaning "inferior substitute" or "replacement." During wartime blockades, Germans used *Ersatz* products — fake coffee, synthetic rubber, substitute everything. English adopted it to describe anything cheap and phony.

**Hinterland.** The remote, less-developed area behind a coast or city. *Hinter* (behind) + *Land* (land). The boondocks. The middle of nowhere.

**Gestalt.** An organized whole that's more than the sum of its parts. Used in psychology, design, and art. *Gestalt* literally means "shape" or "form" in German, but in English it carries the weight of an entire theory of perception.

**Dachshund.** That long-bodied, short-legged wiener dog. *Dachs* (badger) + *Hund* (dog). Badger dog. They were literally bred to hunt badgers. The name tells you everything.

## So How Many German Words Do You Actually Know?

Let's count what we've covered:

Kindergarten, hamburger, frankfurter, pretzel, noodle, angst, schadenfreude, wanderlust, weltanschauung, doppelgänger, poltergeist, kitsch, kaput, rucksack, delicatessen, lager, pilsner, schnitzel, flak, ersatz, hinterland, gestalt, dachshund.

That's **23 German words** you already use in English. And there are more where those came from: *blitzkrieg, Fest, kapellmeister, spiel, wunderkind, zeitgeist, margarine, muesli, spritz, glockenspiel, kirsch, schnaps, fest*...

You're not starting from zero. Not even close.

## Why This Matters for Learning German

Here's the thing most language courses won't tell you: your brain already has hooks for German vocabulary. Every loanword you already know is a free memory anchor. When you learn that *Geist* means "ghost" or "spirit," suddenly *Poltergeist* makes perfect sense. When you learn *Wander* means "to hike," *Wanderlust* stops being a random exotic word and becomes completely transparent.

German isn't as foreign as it looks. English is a Germanic language. They share the same family tree. The grammar is different, sure, but the DNA is related. And every borrowed word is proof.

So the next time someone tells you German is too hard, remind them they've been speaking it for years.
